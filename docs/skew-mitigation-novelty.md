# Predicting mitigation outcomes under skewed Kafka workloads

Literature audit, 30 October 2026. This is a research assessment, not an implemented controller or an additional experiment campaign.

## Scope and conclusion

The research concerns skewed workloads in conventional Kafka consumer groups. Balanced traffic remains a calibration and reference workload. The three intervention families are whole-partition reassignment, consumer scaling, and producer-side hot-key splitting where application semantics permit it. Keeping the current configuration is the control option. Scaling with targeted reassignment combines two of these families; it does not introduce another mechanism.

The focused search did not identify a verified study demonstrating the complete proposed comparison: forecasting recovery and latency outcomes for these permitted actions and unchanged operation from a common pre-intervention skew state, followed by validation of those forecasts in a deployed Kafka consumer group. This is a bounded finding from the sources examined, not proof that no such work exists.

The broad idea of predicting whether a reconfiguration will meet an objective is already established. Ezzeddine uses lag and rate constraints to choose scaling, reassignment or no action. Landau models latency consequences and measures reconfiguration phases. Daedalus and Phoebe explicitly evaluate recovery feasibility for candidate scale-outs. Aj-Stream combines prediction, splitting and resource adaptation. Consequently, neither adding their features to a table nor combining existing mechanisms establishes a new scientific method.

The defensible candidate is a validated method for predicting **which permitted response meets an explicitly defined objective**, including transition behavior and unfinished work, when the existing rules make materially different or unreliable decisions. Whether that method adds value remains to be tested against the closest rules.

## What the comparison means

- **Partition lag** means a per-log-partition backlog observation used by the decision rule. An instance queue or a simulated lag has related information but is identified separately.
- **Arrival and capacity estimation** distinguishes measured rates or estimated service capacity from nominal values supplied to a model. A throughput measurement under light load is not a maximum-capacity estimate.
- **Fixed-count reassignment** means changing whole Kafka partition ownership without increasing consumer count. Key routing, moving an operator between machines, and moving Kafka replicas between brokers are different operations.
- **Splitting** means distributing one original key's future records over multiple processing destinations. A normal partition rebalance or splitting a sentence into words is not hot-key splitting.
- **Outcome prediction** means estimating an intervention's consequences before execution. Forecasting incoming traffic, checking a packing constraint and forecasting a latency distribution are different levels of prediction.
- **Activation delay** runs from the decision/request to an effective configuration. A measured blocking rebalance duration, an assumed startup time and a stabilization wait are recorded separately.
- **Transient p99** means an explicitly defined p99 during the transition or recovery interval. Whole-experiment p99, maximum latency, per-event latency traces and a percentile of rebalance duration do not establish that measurement.
- **Same skew state** requires a common observed starting condition for competing actions: partition backlog and its age/composition, partition input rates, ownership, available capacities and resource settings. Replaying the same input trace is valuable but does not by itself keep the later queue states identical.

## Evidence behind the classifications

**[L] Landau et al., _Latency and cost-aware consumer group autoscaling in message broker systems_, JPDC 201 (2025), 105071.** Sections 4-5 use partition input rates, capacity and the R-score movement proxy. Section 6.3, Equations 16-22, models fixed and moved queues and their latency; Section 6.4 compares algorithms on common rate traces and reports modeled p99. Sections 7-8 establish the Kafka implementation, throughput calibration and separately measured response phases. Section 8 explicitly excludes pod startup time from the principal algorithm comparison. Thus, it would be incorrect to say this work ignores migration cost, lacks latency modeling or never measures startup. It does not demonstrate an online selector forecasting the requested alternatives against one recovery/p99 objective. [Publisher DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpdc.2025.105071); [institutional full text](https://research-portal.uu.nl/files/261656725/1-s2.0-S0743731525000383-main.pdf). Checked PDF pages 4-11, especially 7-8 and 10-11. The 2025 article is the basis here, rather than substituting the earlier 2022/2024 versions.

**[E] Ezzeddine, Baude, Huet and Laaziz, _Latency Aware and Resource-Efficient Bin Pack Autoscaling for Distributed Event Queues: Parameters Impact and Setting_, SN Computer Science 6, 219 (2025).** Equations 1-6 estimate processing capacity, aggregate partition rates/lags by consumer, and constrain packing. Equations 7-8 add arrivals expected during a blocking rebalance. Algorithm 1 and its procedures explicitly include UP, DOWN, REASS and no action. The initial branch selection and subsequent packing with prospective lag are separate stages. Pages 11 and 21 distinguish preparation from rebalance and explain measured versus injected delays. Page 23 says rebalance duration is initialized from previous measurements. This is the strongest direct baseline for lag-sensitive Kafka selection, not merely a resource-threshold autoscaler. [Publisher DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42979-025-03740-9). Checked the complete local 24-page article, with decision rules on pages 6-9 and measurement discussion on pages 11, 20-23. The preceding [2024 paper](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2024/126301/126301.pdf) belongs to the same method family.

**[C] Tadevosyan, Areias and Barbosa, _Towards Heterogeneous Consumer Group Autoscaling for Distributed Event Queues_, IPDPSW (2026), pp. 518-521.** Section III, Equations 1-5 and Algorithms 1-2 combine lag-clearing demand, heterogeneous capacity/cost profiles, assignment and payback guards. Section IV explicitly uses simulation, nominal startup time and a fixed rebalance delay. Its Table III reports average latency and violation duration. The conclusions acknowledge transient violations and leave real-platform validation to future work. The abstract's broad SLA wording must not be read as zero violations in every reported case. [Author page and full text](https://www.dcc.fc.up.pt/~miguel-areias/?page=pubs_2026-INTEL4EC); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/IPDPSW71298.2026.00088). All four pages checked.

**[A] Sun et al., _An adaptive load balancing strategy for stateful join operator in skewed data stream environments_, FGCS 152 (2024), pp. 138-151.** Sections 2-3 distinguish a join-load model, GRU key-frequency prediction, splitting/routing and feedback resource allocation. Equations 13-14 determine and update splitting destinations; Equation 15 detects relatively high instance delay; Equations 16-17 and Algorithm 3 allocate resources using feedback. Section 4 deploys Storm with Kafka input. Its forecasts concern key distribution, rather than a validated recovery/p99 prediction for each Kafka consumer-group action. This nevertheless rules out claiming that combining prediction, splitting and scaling is new. [Author full text](https://clouds.cis.unimelb.edu.au/~rbuyya/papers/DataStreamFGCS2024.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.future.2023.11.002). Methods checked on PDF pages 3-8 and evaluation on pages 8-12.

**[D] Floratou et al., _Dhalion: Self-Regulating Stream Processing in Heron_, PVLDB 10(12) (2017), pp. 1825-1836.** Sections 3 and 5 implement symptom detection, diagnosis, resolver selection and feedback. The resource policy estimates a scale-up factor from backpressure; the skew resolver changes hashing; failed resolutions can be blacklisted. The framework permits other selection techniques, but that extensibility is not an implemented numerical outcome predictor. Section 6 evaluates throughput, skew diagnosis and mixed-fault response sequences. [Primary full text](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol10/p1825-floratou.pdf). Methods checked on PDF pages 4-8 and evaluation on pages 8-12.

## Findings beyond the five-column table

**Daedalus is necessary to assess the proposed recovery claim.** Algorithm 1 tests candidate scale-outs against predicted recovery time and workload capacity. Sections 3.1-3.4 address skew-sensitive capacity, future arrivals and recovery, and the evaluation includes Kafka Streams. Section 4.8 states that latency is not explicitly modeled. This prevents claiming that prediction of recovery under continuing input, skew-aware capacity estimation or choosing unchanged parallelism is new. Its tested decision concerns scale-out, not an independently selectable Kafka ownership or producer-splitting action. [Full paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.02093v2).

**Phoebe is necessary to assess the combined recovery/latency claim.** Sections III-D and III-E and Algorithm 1 assess candidate scale-outs using recovery and average-latency models, then select the smallest feasible scale-out. Its modeled latency is an average, not the proposed transition-window p99. It is a Flink approach and does not demonstrate the three Kafka intervention families. Nevertheless, “predict which configuration meets recovery and latency requirements” alone is already covered. [Author preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.09679); the local ten-page full text was checked.

The wider search also screened the primary publisher records for **MMP-SPS** ([2026 paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.future.2026.108461)) and **Sr-Stream** ([publisher record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0743731526001401)). MMP-SPS selects traffic prediction models and adapts operator replication on Storm. Sr-Stream considers splitting costs and uses input/processing rates and network latency in skew-aware routing. These are relevant overlap checks; neither publisher description establishes the complete proposed Kafka comparison. This screening is not a full algorithm audit of either paper. No exclusion based solely on these descriptions should be presented as definitive.

More generally, model-predictive selection among reconfigurations predates these works; for example, [De Matteis and Mencagli](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0164121216301467) predict future behavior to select core/frequency changes under performance and energy objectives. The novelty claim must concern the specific model and demonstrated decision benefit, not the general predict-and-select pattern.

## What would make the candidate contribution meaningful?

The proposed question is:

> Under skewed Kafka input, can an action-specific model predict which permitted mitigation, or unchanged operation, will meet a predefined recovery and completion-latency objective over a common decision horizon, more reliably than existing lag-aware and movement-aware rules?

This question becomes a contribution only if a justified modeling change improves prediction and selection on independent cases. A missing combination of features, a different platform, or a longer list of metrics is not sufficient evidence.

All candidate forecasts must start from the same information available at decision time. A useful state includes partition backlog and arrival rates, the current ownership map, calibrated consumer capacities, and information about the age and composition of waiting work. Counts and rates alone do not determine end-to-end p99: equally sized queues can contain differently aged messages. The assumed cross-partition service schedule also needs validation.

Each action needs its own forecast of processing during preparation, interruptions affecting particular partitions, and service after activation. These distinctions are established system behavior, not standalone novelty claims. Reassignment changes who processes stored records; producer-side splitting changes future routing and does not relocate the records already stored. For normal Kafka scaling, the predictor must account for uncertainty in the resulting ownership instead of using the realized post-action map as if it were known beforehand.

The recovery deadline, latency cohort/window and allowed unfinished fraction must be specified before judging success. Evaluate all actions to a common horizon from the decision, not a separate horizon starting after each action activates. Report p99 among completed messages with unfinished and deadline outcomes. An action cannot undo a deadline already missed at decision time. If no permitted action meets the objective, the model should report that infeasibility; it must not label no action as successful by default. If unchanged operation meets the objective, avoiding unnecessary transition and resource costs becomes a legitimate choice.

The next test should compare faithful implementations or clearly labeled reconstructions of the existing rules before expanding the experiment campaign. Use controlled skew states and replay common future input, record residual differences in starting conditions, and save each forecast before execution. Measure false feasibility predictions, missed feasible alternatives, recovery/latency prediction error and the cost of the chosen response relative to measured alternatives. Compare a baseline with and without the proposed modeling change using the same implementation of each action. Keep test workloads separate from those used to tune the model, and retain unfavorable results.

There is no requirement to add deep learning. The important result would be a reproducible decision failure of the closest rule and a correction whose benefit survives uncertainty, alternative service schedules and independent workloads. If the baseline performs as well after a fair comparison, the proposed novelty claim must be reduced or withdrawn.

## Relation to the existing proposal and evidence

The supplied proposal table already acknowledges joint provisioning/assignment, coordinated splitting/scaling and adaptive control. It is suitable as a broad related-work overview. Its final column should be read as a **planned comparison**, not an established contribution or completed validation. The new table above audits the narrower forecast question without removing those precedents.

Two wording qualifications are needed. First, “predicting outcomes” must distinguish workload forecasting, feasibility constraints, analytical latency evaluation and validated per-action forecasts. Second, “hold execution mechanisms constant” means using the same implementation of a given action across selector comparisons; it cannot mean that splitting and reassignment have identical mechanisms. No existing results or online proposal text were changed by this audit.

The [decision-rule audit](../experiment-records/decision-rule-audit-20261029/README.md) already shows why a constructed counterexample is insufficient: a candidate check changes its decision quality when the service schedule changes. The [post-mitigation analysis](../experiment-records/post-mitigation-20261029/README.md) also shows that clearing the initial waiting cohort does not establish recovery under continuing input. Those findings motivate the measurements needed for the proposed test; they do not yet validate a new controller. The current saved-data evidence does not substitute for testing hot-key splitting.

The [source manifest](skew-mitigation-novelty-sources.json) identifies the checked PDF versions by SHA-256 and records the depth of the additional online screening. Broker-replica balancing and unrelated application-level uses of Kafka were not treated as consumer-group mitigation evidence. Local full texts supplied evidence where publisher access failed. This was a focused search, not a systematic review of every database, unpublished system or paper; the conclusion is “not identified in the examined evidence,” not “never studied.”
