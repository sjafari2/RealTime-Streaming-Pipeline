\subsection{Twelve high-input partitions: ten-minute evaluation}\label{sec:twelve-partition-updated}\label{sec:four-condition-results}
The twelve-partition comparison used 700 messages/s across three producers and 60 partitions. Twelve selected partitions received 80\% of the input, shared evenly within that set; the other 48 shared the remaining 20\%. All twelve high-traffic partitions initially belonged to Consumer 2. Each run followed the common processing and resource settings in Table~\ref{tab:config}, with one minute of warm-up, ten minutes of evaluation and two minutes of drain. Interventions were requested sixty seconds after evaluation began.

Eight trials compared four responses: no mitigation with three consumers, redistribution within three consumers, scaling to six with targeted redistribution, and scaling to six with Kafka's normal Cooperative Sticky rebalancing. Each response was tested twice, with the order of runs reversed in the second repetition. Workload-generation seeds 81 and 82 reproduced the input pattern within each repetition. Initial ownership and the original consumer pods, nodes and resource declarations were checked before traffic began. Targeted reassignment gave each of three consumers four high-traffic and sixteen other partitions, or each of six consumers two high-traffic and eight other partitions. Normal scaling used Kafka's classic consumer-group protocol and stable consumer identities. Targeted actions used the handover in Section~\ref{sec:targeted-reassignment}. Consequently, this comparison includes both consumer-count and assignment-mechanism differences.

Kafka acknowledged 420,000 evaluation messages in every trial. Keeping the initial assignment left 34.64--34.83\% unfinished at cutoff; all interventions completed these messages. Redistribution within three produced p99 latency of 80.10--84.16 seconds, compared with 100.81--107.24 seconds for targeted scaling and 71.41--72.46 seconds for normal Kafka scaling. The one-second SLO violation rates reveal a different aspect of the latency distribution: 30.50--31.84\% for redistribution, 26.26--27.33\% for targeted scaling and 21.05--25.83\% for normal scaling, versus 83.32--83.36\% without intervention. Thus a higher p99 does not imply more misses at every deadline.

The resource measurements show the trade-off. Redistribution used approximately 1,035--1,038 observed consumer CPU core-seconds over evaluation plus drain; targeted scaling used 1,310--1,327 and normal scaling 1,452--1,459. Requested CPU was 72 core-minutes for redistribution and approximately 137 for either six-consumer response. Actual RSS integrals were 2.53--2.65, 3.95--4.15 and 3.66--3.69 GiB-minutes respectively. These values concern consumer processes and resource declarations, not total infrastructure cost. The first unchanged baseline lacks almost all local request history and cannot support a full-window request-cost comparison; its message outcomes and recovered historical process measurements remain usable within their stated coverage.

Targeted scaling confirmed recovery sooner than redistribution within three (169.0--184.9 versus 305.2--328.9 seconds after the action decision), despite its higher p99 across completed evaluation messages. Recovery requires processing backlog at most 100 offsets for thirty consecutive valid seconds while input continues. Normal scaling's recovery varied from 143.0 to 259.0 seconds. Explicit ownership changes use a global release/acquire/resume barrier; their 14.40--14.54-second and 19.05--19.50-second processing pauses are distinguished from complete action duration. Normal cooperative scaling had much shorter global gaps, which does not imply every partition processed continuously.

Persistent hotspots were present before intervention. In the redistribution and targeted-scaling runs, none remained at any defined snapshot in the final two evaluation minutes. The diagnostic figure also shows that relative skew may rise during successful backlog clearance: the maximum and mean absolute partition lags are then small. Persistence and skew are useful descriptions of concentration, but neither alone establishes overload.

Every trial achieved 700 acknowledged evaluation messages/s. No duplicate completions were detected.
Observed per-process CPU coverage over evaluation plus drain ranged from 87.50\% to 99.72\%. Coverage uses the complete observation window, including time before additional consumers started; a lower value for a newly started consumer is not automatically a monitoring failure. The integral includes only observed valid process intervals.
The tables distinguish action timing from recovery. \emph{Decision to active} runs from the action decision to confirmation of the intended partition ownership. For targeted reassignment, the transfer interval starts when release is requested; the processing pause can be shorter because processing may resume before ownership verification finishes. \emph{Global gap} is the longest interval with no consumer processing, rather than the downtime of every partition. Extra pending work is measured over the pause, and net pending change over the transfer; a negative change means processing reduced unfinished work during that interval. Recovery is measured from the action decision to confirmation of the low-backlog criterion. Dashes identify unavailable or inapplicable measurements.

For normal Kafka scaling, active ownership is confirmed when all six consumers have stable assignments. The added-consumer delay table measures the first valid completion on each new consumer, using the replica-request timestamp for targeted scaling and the action-decision timestamp for normal scaling, where an equivalent request timestamp was not separately recorded. These activation delays include startup and processing, so they are not pure rebalance durations. Tables~\ref{tab:combined-partition-ownership} and~\ref{tab:combined-activation-delay} combine the assignments and added-consumer delays for this workload and the four-partition follow-up in Section~\ref{sec:four-partition-updated}. C0--C5 identify consumers; the assignment counts refer to the designated high-traffic partitions. A dash means no observed assignment. A row labelled 1 and 2 records the same assignment in both runs.

The lag tables use time-weighted means over valid intervals and the largest observed values as maxima. Lag is reported in offsets, skew as a ratio, and coverage as a percentage. Mean skew averages the ratio at each observation, rather than dividing two run-level means. Mean completion latency and p99 use evaluation messages completed by the end of drain. Actual CPU and resident-memory totals sum observed consumer-process measurements over time, while requested-resource totals use Kubernetes declarations. A dash in a resource table indicates missing full-period measurements, not zero resource use.

\paragraph{Performance and resource use after recovery.}
Table~\ref{tab:four-condition-after-recovery} compares messages produced during evaluation minutes eight to ten, after both redistribution and targeted scaling had met the recovery criterion. Input continued during this interval; the two-minute drain followed evaluation. Each run contributed 84,000 acknowledged messages, all completed by the original drain cutoff. This interval and its one-second SLO comparison were selected after the trials and supplement the full-evaluation results. CPU use and resident memory are time-weighted means for each consumer, summed across consumers, with 98.3\% measurement coverage. They represent actual use, not Kubernetes resource requests.

Redistribution within three consumers had lower p99 and used less CPU and memory in both repetitions during this interval. Both responses sustained approximately 700 completions/s with no persistent hot partitions. Targeted scaling therefore cleared accumulated work sooner but did not give later messages lower p99. SLO violations showed a different pattern: the second targeted-scaling run had none, whereas the corresponding redistribution run had eighteen. These observations do not establish that either response is preferable for every metric.
